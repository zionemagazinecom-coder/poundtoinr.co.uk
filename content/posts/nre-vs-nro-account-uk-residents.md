# NRE vs NRO Account for UK Residents: Key Differences

## Quick comparison

The main difference between an **NRE vs NRO account for UK residents** is the intended source and treatment of the money. An NRE account is generally used for eligible overseas funds remitted to India. An NRO account is commonly used for legitimate rupee transactions and income arising in India.

Both are Indian rupee accounts. They are not interchangeable labels, and the correct account depends on residential status, the source of funds and what the account holder plans to do with the money.

Reserve Bank of India guidance dated January 2025 lists NRE, FCNR(B) and NRO as major account types available under the non-resident framework. It sets different rules for who may open them, permitted transactions and repatriation.

This article provides general information, not individual banking, legal or tax advice. The account holder should confirm current eligibility and documentation with an authorised Indian bank.

## NRE account

NRE means Non-Resident External Rupee Account. RBI guidance says NRE accounts may be opened by NRIs and PIOs, with PIO treatment including eligible OCI cardholders resident outside India.

The account is denominated in Indian rupees even though money may arrive from outside India in foreign currency. When pounds are remitted from the UK, the bank or transfer route converts them into INR before or when crediting the account.

NRE accounts are commonly used for permitted foreign earnings, savings or other eligible overseas funds sent to India. Permitted transfers from other non-resident accounts may also be possible under applicable rules.

The RBI describes NRE balances as repatriable. In practical terms, eligible principal and interest can generally be transferred abroad, subject to regulatory, banking, identification and transaction checks.

Repatriable does not mean every transfer is instant or exempt from review. Banks still apply know-your-customer, anti-money-laundering and source-of-funds procedures.

## NRO account

NRO means Non-Resident Ordinary Rupee Account. It is generally used for bona fide rupee transactions and income connected with India.

Examples may include rent from Indian property, pension, dividends, local payments or proceeds that are permitted under the account rules. The exact credit or debit still needs to comply with current regulation and the bank's controls.

RBI guidance says any person resident outside India may open an NRO account for bona fide rupee transactions, subject to specified restrictions and approval requirements for certain nationalities or circumstances.

NRO balances have different repatriation treatment from NRE balances. Current income may generally be remitted subject to applicable requirements, while other eligible balances can be subject to limits, tax documentation and banking procedures.

The RBI's January 2025 FAQ refers to remittances by NRIs and PIOs from NRO balances up to USD 1 million per financial year under the relevant FEMA provisions. That figure should not be treated as automatic approval: eligibility, supporting documents and taxes still matter.

## Who can open them

Eligibility begins with residential status under Indian foreign-exchange rules, not simply nationality or possession of a UK address.

The RBI defines an NRI for this purpose as an Indian citizen resident outside India. It also describes the PIO category and includes eligible <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-resident_Indian_and_Overseas_Citizen_of_India" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">OCI cardholders within non-resident Indian account terminology</a>, provided the relevant conditions are met.

A UK resident who is an eligible NRI, PIO or OCI may be able to open an NRE account. NRO eligibility is broader in wording, but restrictions and bank checks still apply.

Someone who has returned to India or changed residential status should tell the bank. RBI guidance explains that NRE accounts may need redesignation or transfer to an eligible resident foreign-currency account, while NRO accounts may also need redesignation when status changes.

Do not keep using an old account classification without informing the bank of a permanent move.

## Money sources

The source of funds is often the clearest starting point.

If the money is salary or savings earned in the UK and the account holder is eligible, an NRE account may fit the purpose. The funds enter through normal banking channels and are converted into rupees.

If the money is rent, pension, dividends or other legitimate income arising in India, an NRO account is commonly the relevant home for it.

The distinction prevents overseas funds and Indian-source rupee income from being treated as though they have identical regulatory and tax characteristics.

Some situations involve both sources. An eligible person may therefore maintain both account types rather than forcing every receipt through one account.

Ask the bank how a particular receipt should be classified before moving it. A familiar label is not a substitute for checking the actual source and purpose.

## Repatriation

Repatriation means transferring eligible money from India to another country, such as returning funds to a UK account.

NRE balances are generally described as freely repatriable under the applicable framework. That feature is one reason eligible overseas earners may use an NRE account for foreign funds converted into rupees.

NRO repatriation is more conditional. Current income and other eligible amounts can be treated differently, and documentary requirements may include tax evidence or declarations.

The often-quoted USD 1 million annual provision for eligible NRO remittances is a regulatory ceiling under stated conditions, not a promise that every balance can be sent abroad without review.

Ask the bank which documents apply to the exact transaction. Property proceeds, inherited assets, rent and ordinary savings can require different evidence.

Do not split transfers to avoid checks or reporting. Keep a clean record of where the money came from and why it is being moved.

## Indian tax treatment

Indian tax treatment is another important difference, but account labels alone do not settle every tax question.

Interest on qualifying NRE deposits is generally described as exempt from Indian income tax while the conditions for the exemption are met. NRO interest is generally taxable in India and banks may deduct tax at source.

Rates, reliefs and eligibility depend on current law and the account holder's facts. A bank's tax deduction does not always equal the final tax liability.

Income such as rent, dividends, capital gains or pension can have its own treatment regardless of the account receiving it.

Keep interest certificates, tax deduction records and account statements. They may be needed for an Indian return, a refund claim, treaty relief or UK reporting.

For material sums or mixed sources, obtain advice from a professional familiar with both Indian and UK rules.

## UK tax treatment

An Indian exemption does not automatically make income tax-free in the United Kingdom.

HMRC guidance says UK residents are generally taxed on foreign income under the applicable UK rules, unless a specific relief applies. Foreign bank interest can therefore be relevant even when it remains in an NRE account in India.

The UK changed its foreign income and gains regime from 6 April 2025. A residence-based four-year relief may apply to qualifying new residents who meet the detailed conditions, while people outside that regime generally need to consider worldwide income as it arises.

These rules are fact-sensitive. The date someone became UK resident, their previous residence history, amount and type of income, and any double-taxation relief can all matter.

Do not assume old “non-dom” summaries still describe the current position. Keep annual interest and tax records and check the latest HMRC guidance or obtain professional advice.

## Exchange-rate risk

Both NRE and NRO accounts hold rupees. A UK resident measuring wealth or spending in pounds therefore faces GBP/INR exchange-rate risk.

Suppose GBP strengthens against INR after pounds have been converted and deposited. The same rupee balance would then be worth fewer pounds when converted back, even if the account earned interest.

If GBP weakens, the rupee balance may be worth more pounds. Neither direction is guaranteed.

Interest rates should therefore not be compared without considering currency movement, tax and transfer costs.

## Fees and margins

The cost of funding an Indian account can include more than one charge.

A provider may apply an exchange-rate margin, transfer fee, card funding charge or speed premium. A receiving bank or intermediary may also have relevant charges depending on the route.

Compare providers using the final INR amount credited for the same number of pounds. The <a href="/guides/uk-to-india-money-transfer-fees/">UK to India transfer-fee guide</a> explains how a “zero fee” service can still make money through its customer rate.

For larger transfers, a small rate margin can cost more than a visible fixed fee. For smaller transfers, the fixed fee can dominate.

Keep the quote screen or confirmation showing the rate, fees and payout. It creates a useful record if the amount credited differs from what was promised.

## Documents

Banks commonly require proof of identity, overseas address, Indian status and tax identifiers where applicable. They may also request a photograph, passport, visa or residence evidence and proof of the source of funds.

Requirements vary by bank, account type and transaction. Online onboarding may be available, but original, certified or notarised documents can still be required in some cases.

Names and addresses should be consistent across documents. An outdated address or different spelling can delay onboarding or a transfer review.

For an existing account, keep know-your-customer information current. A bank can restrict activity when mandatory updates are overdue.

Never send identity documents to an unverified email address or messaging account. Use the bank's official website, app, branch or confirmed secure channel.

## Choosing the account

Choose an NRE account when you are eligible, the funds are permitted overseas money and repatriability is an important objective.

Choose an NRO account when the account needs to receive or manage legitimate Indian-source rupee income and local transactions under the applicable rules.

Consider holding both when you have both overseas earnings and Indian-source income that should remain clearly separated.

Do not choose solely on the advertised interest rate. Compare account access, minimum-balance requirements, service charges, deposit protection, online security, tax treatment and currency exposure.

## Common mistakes

One mistake is sending Indian rent into an NRE account without checking whether that credit is permitted. Another is sending overseas savings into an account merely because its number was already available.

Using a resident savings account after becoming non-resident can also create problems. Residential status changes should be reported to the bank promptly.

People sometimes focus on Indian tax exemption while ignoring possible UK reporting. The two countries apply their own rules, and treaty relief does not remove the need for proper records.

Another error is comparing interest rates without measuring GBP/INR risk. A higher rupee return can be offset by exchange-rate movement and conversion costs.

Finally, do not treat the word “repatriable” as permission to move unexplained funds. Banks still need evidence supporting the source and purpose.

## Practical checklist

Before opening or using either account, confirm these points:

Check your current residential and NRI, PIO or OCI eligibility status.

Identify whether the funds arise in the UK, India or another country.

Ask the bank which credits and debits are permitted.

Confirm repatriation conditions for the exact source of money.

Compare the final GBP/INR payout and all transfer fees.

Understand Indian tax deduction and UK reporting implications.

Keep source-of-funds, interest and tax documents.

Update the bank when your address or residential status changes.

This checklist is more reliable than choosing an account from a short marketing comparison.

## Common questions

**Can a UK resident have both NRE and NRO accounts?**

An eligible non-resident may commonly maintain both because they serve different types of funds. The bank should confirm individual eligibility and permitted use.

**Which account should receive UK salary savings?**

For an eligible account holder, permitted overseas earnings are commonly remitted to an NRE account. Confirm the purpose and routing with the receiving bank.

**Which account should receive rent from India?**

Indian-source income such as rent is commonly associated with an NRO account. Tax and documentation requirements still apply.

**Is NRE interest tax-free for a UK resident?**

It may qualify for exemption in India while the relevant conditions are met, but UK tax rules can still apply to a UK resident's foreign interest.

**Can NRO money be sent back to the UK?**

Eligible amounts may be repatriated subject to limits, taxes, documentation and bank checks. The source of funds determines what evidence is needed.

## Final view

The practical answer to **NRE vs NRO account for UK residents** depends on where the money comes from and what the holder needs to do with it.

NRE is generally suited to eligible overseas funds and offers broader repatriability. NRO is generally suited to Indian-source income and local rupee transactions, with more conditional repatriation.

Confirm the account choice with the bank, compare conversion costs using a transparent reference rate, and consider both Indian and UK tax obligations before moving significant sums.
